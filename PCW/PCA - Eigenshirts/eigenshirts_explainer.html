Eigenshirts, Explained

How 1,000 tiny clothing pictures get boiled down to a handful of "ingredient" images, and what those ingredients actually are. Every figure on this page uses the real numbers from your notebook.

Step 1 · A picture is a list of numbers

Each image is one point in 1,024-dimensional space

Your notebook turns every picture into a 32 × 32 grid of brightness values between 0 (black) and 1 (white). Then reshape(len(images), -1) lays the 32 rows end to end into one row of 1,024 numbers.

So each shirt is a point with 1,024 coordinates. You can't picture 1,024 axes, but the maths is the same as for 2 or 3 axes.

↑ The same shirt flattened: all 1,024 numbers in one row, left to right (rows 1, 2, 3 … of the image).

Step 2 · What an eigenvector is

The direction your data stretches the most

Start small: 2 dimensions instead of 1,024. Each dot below is a data point. Turn the line and watch the spread of the dots along it (the variance of their projections). One angle gives the biggest spread. That direction is the first eigenvector of the covariance matrix C, and the spread along it is its eigenvalue λ. It's the solution of C v = λ v.

spread along line max possible

Eigenvector 2 is at 90° to eigenvector 1 and holds whatever spread is left. With 1,024 dimensions there are up to 1,024 such directions, each at right angles to all the earlier ones, each with less spread than the one before.

Step 3 · Eigenvectors of shirts

Each eigenvector is itself a 1,024-number "image"

In 1,024 dimensions an eigenvector is a list of 1,024 weights, one per pixel. Fold it back into a 32 × 32 grid and you can look at it: that's an eigenshirt. Teal pixels get added, magenta pixels get taken away, and neutral pixels don't change.

Reading them: the mean is the "average garment". PC 1 is mostly overall brightness of the body (a light vs dark top). PC 2 contrasts the body with long sleeves, which is exactly the T-shirt vs shirt difference. PC 3 swaps between sleeve shapes. Later ones pick out smaller details: collars, hems, button plackets, patterns.

Step 5 · Your question

Going up means more dimensions, not more runs

When the notebook goes from 5 to 20 to 28 components, it is not training again or running PCA more times to get a "better" answer. PCA is solved once, exactly: one eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix gives every eigenvector, already sorted from most to least spread. Choosing k only decides how many of those directions you keep, which is the number of dimensions in your new coordinate system.

keach image becomesvariance keptwhat you see

Why the pictures look different with more PCs: each extra eigenshirt adds one more layer of detail on top of what's there. PC 1 alone only knows "light or dark top". Add PC 2 and the sleeves appear. By about 20 the collar and hem show up. The first eigenshirts never change when you add more: PCA(5) gives exactly the same 5 eigenshirts as the first 5 of PCA(60). The notebook calls PCA(n) separately for each n only for convenience.

Why the later eigenshirts look like noise: they carry tiny amounts of variance (fractions of a percent), so they describe fine pixel-level wiggles rather than shapes. That's why you can drop them: 28 of 1,024 dimensions already keep 90% of the variance.

Step 6 · Why this helps the classifier

In the first two dimensions, the classes already pull apart

Here are 500 of the images plotted by their first two coefficients (c₁, c₂). Even with only 2 of 1,024 numbers, T-shirts and shirts sit in mostly different regions. That's why OLS and logistic regression reach about 78% accuracy with just 2 components and about 80% with 10–28. The overlap in the middle is the shirts that look like T-shirts.

Summary

The whole idea in five lines

  1. Each image is a point with 1,024 coordinates (one per pixel).
  2. The covariance matrix's eigenvectors are the directions those points spread out most, sorted by eigenvalue (amount of spread).
  3. Folded back to 32 × 32, the eigenvectors are eigenshirts: ingredient images.
  4. Keeping k of them means describing each image with k numbers instead of 1,024. More k means more dimensions and more detail, not more runs.
  5. k = 28 keeps 90% of the variance, so the images stay recognizable at about 37× compression.
