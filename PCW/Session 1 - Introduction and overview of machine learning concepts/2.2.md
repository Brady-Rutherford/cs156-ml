**User** *(attached: screenshot of equation 1.1 from Murphy)*

> For example, suppose we create the following decision rule:
>
> f(x; θ) = { Setosa if petal length < 2.45 ; Versicolor or Virginica otherwise }   (1.1)

how would i read this

**Assistant**

Read it as: f of x given theta.

Piece by piece:

- **f** is the classifier, a function that outputs a class label.
- **x** (bold) is the input, a vector of features for one flower: petal length, petal width, sepal length, sepal width.
- **;** separates the input from the parameters. Everything before the semicolon is data you feed in, everything after is what defines the rule itself.
- **θ** (bold) is the parameter vector. Here it encodes the choices that make this specific rule: which feature to split on (petal length) and the threshold (2.45). A different θ gives a different rule from the same family.
- **{** is a piecewise definition. Check the top condition first, fall through to the bottom otherwise.

So out loud: f of x given theta returns Setosa if the petal length of x is less than 2.45, and Versicolor or Virginica otherwise.

The point of writing it with θ rather than just hardcoding 2.45 is that learning becomes a search over θ. The shape of the rule is fixed; training picks the numbers.

**Assistant**

Welcome to the course. Before we touch a single model, I want to give you the four load-bearing ideas, because everything on the syllabus from naive Bayes to transformers is a recombination of them. If you build the mind map correctly, the six edges will matter more to you than the four nodes.

#### Node 1: Uncertainty and probabilistic modeling

The commitment that every unknown quantity, including model parameters, is a random variable with a distribution over its possible values.

- Sub-branches: epistemic vs aleatoric uncertainty; the frequentist vs Bayesian reading of probability; conditional models $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ vs unconditional models $p(\boldsymbol{x})$; priors, likelihoods, posteriors
- Where it lands on our syllabus: Bayesian statistics, naive Bayes, hidden Markov models, probabilistic PCA, the softmax output of every classifier we build

#### Node 2: Representation and inductive bias

The set of functions your model is allowed to express, and the assumptions baked into that choice before any data arrives.

- Sub-branches: hypothesis space; feature maps $\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})$, whether hand-designed or learned; capacity and parameter count; conditional independence structure; the no free lunch theorem
- Where it lands: linear parameter models, kernels, decision trees, PCA and autoencoders, the architecture choices in deep nets and transformers

#### Node 3: Objectives and optimization

The scalar that defines what good means, and the procedure that searches parameter space for it.

- Sub-branches: loss $\ell(y,\hat{y})$; empirical risk $L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \ell(y_n, f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))$; maximum likelihood; convexity; gradients and backpropagation; gradient descent and its variants
- Where it lands: multivariate calculus for gradients, MLE, perceptron learning, SVM as a constrained optimization problem, training deep networks

#### Node 4: Generalization

The recognition that performance on data you already have is not the goal, and the machinery for estimating performance on data you do not.

- Sub-branches: population risk $L(\boldsymbol{\theta};p^*) = \mathbb{E}_{p^*}[\ell(y,f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}))]$; the generalization gap; overfitting and underfitting; bias-variance tradeoff; train/validation/test protocol; regularization; cross-validation
- Where it lands: every model selection decision you make this semester, tree depth, network size, kernel bandwidth, number of principal components

---

#### The six edges

##### Edge A: Uncertainty ↔ Representation

*Probability turns a function class into a distribution family, and makes the inductive bias explicit.*

- A deterministic $f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ becomes $p(y\mid\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ by wrapping it in a noise model. Gaussian noise for regression, softmax for classification.
- Structural assumptions become probabilistic statements you can write down and test. Naive Bayes is exactly the claim $p(\boldsymbol{x}\mid y) = \prod_d p(x_d \mid y)$. That is an inductive bias in the form of a conditional independence.
- A prior over parameters $p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is inductive bias in its most explicit form: a statement about which functions you consider plausible before seeing data. A kernel plays the same role, defining a prior over functions through a similarity measure.

##### Edge B: Uncertainty ↔ Objectives

*The probability model generates the loss function. You do not choose them independently.*

- Set $\ell(y, f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})) = -\log p(y \mid f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}))$ and empirical risk minimization becomes maximum likelihood estimation.
- Two consequences you should verify yourself this week: Gaussian likelihood gives $\text{NLL} = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{MSE} + \text{const}$, so least squares is MLE under Gaussian noise. Categorical likelihood gives cross-entropy.
- Running the same argument on the posterior instead of the likelihood turns $L_2$ regularization into a Gaussian prior and $L_1$ into a Laplace prior. Regularization is not a hack, it is MAP estimation.
- Decision theory closes the loop in the other direction: given a posterior and an asymmetric loss matrix, the optimal action is not always the most probable class.

##### Edge C: Uncertainty ↔ Generalization

*Generalization is defined as an expectation, so it is a probabilistic object by construction.*

- Population risk is an expectation under the true distribution $p^*$ that you never observe. Test risk is a Monte Carlo estimate of it, which is why test set size determines how much you should trust it.
- The epistemic/aleatoric split tells you whether a generalization failure is fixable. More data reduces epistemic uncertainty and does nothing for aleatoric.
- Averaging over the posterior rather than committing to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{mle}}$ is itself a defense against overfitting. Bayesian model averaging and ensembling are the same idea in different dress.
- Calibration is a generalization property that accuracy cannot see. A model can be right 90% of the time and badly wrong about how confident it should be.

##### Edge D: Representation ↔ Objectives

*The representation determines the geometry of the loss surface, which determines whether optimization is easy or hard.*

- This is the pair students underestimate. A model linear in its parameters, even with a wildly nonlinear $\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})$, produces a convex MSE with a unique global optimum and a closed-form solution.
- Make $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ itself parameterized, as in a deep network, and you lose convexity entirely. Backpropagation and stochastic gradient descent exist because of that specific choice of representation.
- The kernel trick is the sharpest illustration: it buys you an implicitly infinite-dimensional feature space while keeping the SVM objective a convex quadratic program. Representation and tractability, negotiated.
- Directionality runs both ways. Sometimes we choose an architecture because it is optimizable, not because it is expressive.

##### Edge E: Representation ↔ Generalization

*Capacity is the primary control knob on the generalization gap.*

- Polynomial degree, tree depth, network width, number of retained principal components. Different names, same knob. Turn it up and training error falls monotonically while test error traces a U.
- No free lunch means there is no universally correct setting, because an inductive bias that helps on images hurts on tabular data. The bias must match the domain.
- Feature learning changes the tradeoff rather than just navigating it. A good learned representation from an autoencoder or a pretrained transformer improves sample efficiency downstream, meaning you need fewer labels for the same test error.
- Dimensionality reduction is regularization by another route: PCA constrains the hypothesis space by discarding directions of low variance before the model ever sees the data.

##### Edge F: Objectives ↔ Generalization

*You minimize the objective you can compute and want the one you cannot. That gap is the whole discipline.*

- ERM targets $L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})$; you care about $L(\boldsymbol{\theta};p^*)$. Everything else in this edge is a response to that mismatch.
- Regularization modifies the objective deliberately so that its minimizer generalizes better, accepting worse training loss in exchange.
- Model selection requires a third data split. Train fits parameters, validation chooses among models, test estimates future performance and is touched by neither.
- Optimization carries its own implicit bias: early stopping limits effective capacity, and SGD's noise favors certain solutions over others among the many that fit the training data equally well. How you search changes what you find.

---

Two things to hold onto as you build the map.

First, this is one defensible carving, not the carving. Murphy's Chapter 1 organizes the field differently, by type of supervision: supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement. That taxonomy answers what training signal do you have, while mine answers what decisions must you make to build a model. Both are correct and they cross-cut each other, which is a useful thing to notice when you compare this outline against the reading.

Second, when you sit down with the material, work Edge D and Edge F hardest. Edges B and C tend to click on first contact. D and F are where the semester's genuine difficulty lives.

### Turn 5

**User**

In 2026 do you believe this is all still relevant?

**Assistant**

Mostly yes, but I would be doing you a disservice if I said yes without qualification. Let me separate what has held up from what has genuinely aged.

**What is still solid.** The four-node decomposition itself. Every system built today still requires you to decide what function class to search, what scalar defines good, how you handle uncertainty in the output, and how you know it works on data you have not seen. Transformers did not repeal any of that. The MLE-equals-cross-entropy identity is exactly what a language model minimizes during pretraining, so Edge B is arguably more central now than it was in 2012.

**What has genuinely been contested.**

*The U-shaped test error curve.* This is the one I would flag hardest. The classical picture, where test error rises monotonically once you pass the sweet spot, is incomplete. Heavily overparameterized networks routinely interpolate the training set and then continue to improve on test data as you add parameters. The phenomenon has a name, double descent, and it means the bias-variance story as usually taught does not describe the regime most modern models operate in. Murphy's Chapter 1 presents the classical curve without this caveat.

*Capacity as the control knob.* Parameter count turns out to be a poor proxy for effective capacity. What actually constrains the solution is a mix of architecture, optimizer dynamics, and stopping time. Edge F's point about implicit bias in optimization has grown from a footnote into a large part of the explanation.

*No free lunch.* Still true as a theorem, increasingly misleading as guidance. The transformer is one architecture that works across text, images, audio, protein sequences, and time series. That is not what the field expected in 2012. The prevailing view now leans toward general architectures plus scale rather than carefully matched domain-specific inductive bias.

*Train/validation/test discipline.* Sound in principle, hard to enforce in practice. When a model is pretrained on a large fraction of the public internet, guaranteeing that your test set was not seen during training is genuinely difficult, and benchmark contamination is a live problem rather than a hypothetical one.

**What the framing is missing.** If I were building this map from scratch today, I would add a fifth node: scale. Data quantity, data quality, and compute allocation now behave as first-class design variables with their own empirical regularities, and they connect to all four existing nodes. Nothing in Murphy's Chapter 1 prepares you for the observation that many problems are solved by more data and compute rather than by a better model.

**On your specific syllabus.** Kernels, SVMs, and HMMs are much less common in production than they were fifteen years ago. I would still teach them, and not only for history. SVMs are the cleanest example of Edge D you will ever see, kernels make explicit what a learned representation does implicitly, and HMMs are the right stepping stone to attention because they force you to think about what it means to condition on a sequence. Learn them as instruments for understanding the edges, not as tools you expect to deploy.

The honest summary is that the vocabulary and the four decisions survive. Several of the specific empirical claims attached to them, particularly around generalization, are actively contested, and you should read Chapter 1's treatment of overfitting as the classical position rather than the settled one.

Pair plots inspect distributions, residual plots diagnose the objective, learning curves are pure generalization, and t-SNE or UMAP plots interrogate a learned representation.
