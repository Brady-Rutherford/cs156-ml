# Model the hazard, feed it classroom cues

Brady's best bet is not a deeper text network. It is a **discrete-time hazard model**: logistic regression and LightGBM fitted to a 2-second class-period table whose features combine his own raise history, class timing, and a handful of transcript cues that are timestamped causally. In statistics this is a point-process GLM, and for each horizon H the "hand up within H seconds" classifier is its close relative, the landmark model ([Truccolo et al. 2005](https://web.math.ku.dk/~susanne/BSc/TruccoloEdenFellowsDonoghueBrown2005.pdf); [van Houwelingen 2007](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9469.2006.00529.x)). Text pays off in two ways: as a few low-dimensional signals (the professor just asked an open question, a classmate was cold-called, the topic matches his workbook answers), and as out-of-fold scores from TF-IDF and sentence-embedding models stacked into the tree model, the arrangement that won an 18-dataset text-plus-tabular benchmark ([Shi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02705)). Of the ten planned models, the evidence backs four as written: the base rate, multinomial naive Bayes, TF-IDF logistic regression and LightGBM. The time-only logistic regression should become the hazard GLM, bge-small-en-v1.5 should replace MiniLM as the default embedder, and the GRU, TextCNN, kNN and DistilBERT should shrink to optional ablations. The most useful additions are question and invitation detectors (including Stanford's September 2026 EduBehaviors classifiers), a topic-relevance feature, out-of-fold stacking, explicit multi-horizon outputs, calibration grouped by session, and an event-level alarm metric with session-bootstrap intervals. Neural temporal point processes ignore the transcript, TurnGPT and Voice Activity Projection need audio or retraining, DeepSurv-style survival networks assume one event per subject, and LLM fine-tuning will not fit on an M4 this week. The evidence is thinnest where it matters most: no published work forecasts a student's hand raise from classroom talk, every off-the-shelf discourse classifier was trained on K-12 human transcripts, and the M4 timings come from one local benchmark. The realistic deliverable for Friday is a leakage-audited hazard-plus-cues model with honest uncertainty, whose most interesting result will be how much the transcript adds over timing alone.

## No published model forecasts hand raises from talk, so baselines must earn every gain

The research found **no study that predicts when a student will raise a hand from classroom discourse or timing**. Classroom machine learning on hand-raising *detects* the gesture in video ([Computers & Graphics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849322002047)). The nearest forecasting analogue, FairTalk, predicts "turn-grabbing intention" in video calls using positive-unlabelled learning with detected turn-takes as positives, and its abstract gives neither its inputs nor its accuracy ([Iijima et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20138)). Brady's task therefore has no published benchmark: simple baselines are the only yardstick, and his numbers will be the first reported for this setting.

Education research does say clearly where the signal lives. Hand-raising is "**commonly prompted by a specific learning task (i.e., teacher question)**" ([Böheim et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X2030059X), abstract level). Teachers' natural pause after a question averages **2.04 s (range 0.97–3.70 s)**, and stretching it past 3 s draws more voluntary responses, including from usually silent students ([Shiau et al. 2024 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11545128/)). A longitudinal video study attributes **nearly half of hand-raising variance to stable differences between students** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376076635_A_Longitudinal_Study_of_Student_Hand_Raising_Stability_and_Reciprocal_Dynamics_with_Cognitive_Elaboration_and_Academic_Self-Concept); search-snippet evidence, authors unconfirmed). For a single student that trait collapses into an intercept, so what remains to model is the situation (questions, pauses, topic) and his own history (raises so far, time since the last one). Minerva's Talk Time panel colour-codes each student's share of speaking time and is designed to help instructors "decide which student to call on" ([Minerva Help Center](https://help.minervaproject.com/en/articles/4296097-talk-time)). That creates a plausible confound: if a professor cold-calls him because his share is low, he has less reason to raise. His cumulative talk time, computable causally from speaker tags, belongs in the feature set as a hypothesis to test.

Turn-taking research sets sober expectations for text. In a continuous turn-taking model, **word features alone reached F ≈ 0.71 at pauses against 0.78 for acoustic-plus-word features** ([Roddy et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.11461)), so text carries real but weaker signal than audio. TurnGPT's balanced accuracy fell from 0.913 on assistant dialogue to **0.823 on spontaneous speech**, and its biggest loss came from removing all context ([Ekstedt & Skantze 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.10874)). Simple heuristics are hard to beat: on AMI meetings, a zero-shot Llama 8B scored **34.88% next-speaker accuracy against 33.41% for "the last speaker continues"**, and only LoRA fine-tuning lifted it, to 47.85% ([Hilgert & Niehues 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.icnlsp-1.7.pdf)). Zero-shot LLMs likewise "failed consistently" at deciding when to speak in multi-party talk ([Bhagtani et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11409)). Short and long context also answer different questions. Turn-change prediction peaks with about five utterances of context, while next-speaker identity keeps improving to about forty ([Fukuda et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17542v1)). That argues for short-window "is the floor opening?" features plus longer, class-level "is it him?" features. Dyadic speech gaps cluster near 200 ms ([Stivers et al. 2009](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0903616106)), but a hand raise is a deliberate bid to the chair, often made while someone else is still talking, so those norms are background rather than an expected latency.

Taken together, the base rate, an empirical hazard by time since his last raise, and a "professor asked a question in the last k seconds" rule are serious competitors. A reasonable target, inferred from this literature rather than measured anywhere, is **average precision a few times the base rate, not near-perfect discrimination**.

## A 2-second hazard table is the backbone, and the H sweep falls out of it

Turn every class into one row per 2-second decision point while Brady is "at risk" (hand down, not speaking), label the row 1 if the icon appears in the next step, and fit logistic regression. That is the textbook **person-period discrete-time hazard model** ([Singer & Willett](https://gseacademic.harvard.edu/~willetjo/dsta.htm)). Add his own raise history as covariates (log time since last raise, two or three exponentially decayed sums of past raises, a capped count so far) and it becomes the **point-process GLM** that neuroscience uses for spike trains driven by their own history plus external inputs ([Truccolo et al. 2005](https://web.math.ku.dk/~susanne/BSc/TruccoloEdenFellowsDonoghueBrown2005.pdf)). This is the "beyond intro" version of the planned time-only logistic regression, and it costs the same to fit. Because history coefficients can be negative, it can learn a refractory dip right after a raise and a soft cap on raises per class. Classical Hawkes models cannot, because their positivity constraints "fail to capture inhibition effects" ([Mei & Eisner 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.09328)).

The H sweep follows from the same table. If h is the per-step hazard, then P(raise within H) = 1 − ∏(1 − h) over the next H/2 steps. That identity needs covariates for future steps, which include transcript not yet spoken, so **computing it exactly leaks the future**. There are two causal routes. Freezing covariates at their time-t values and rolling only the history clock forward gives a monotone p_H for every H from one model, at the cost of an approximation. Fitting a separate classifier for each H gives the **landmark model** of dynamic prediction, a published method rather than a hack ([van Houwelingen 2007](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9469.2006.00529.x); [dynamicLM tutorial](https://github.com/thehanlab/dynamicLM/blob/main/tutorials/theory-landmark-supermodel.md)). Report both, and expect the landmark model to win at large H, where frozen covariates handicap the hazard model most. Landmark rows 2 s apart share overlapping labels and are heavily autocorrelated, while single-step hazard rows are not. Either way, every split, interval and test must treat the class session as the unit.

The GLM should hold only a few dozen effective parameters with L2 regularisation. Time since last raise enters as a spline or quantile bins; quantile grids beat equidistant ones on small discrete-time survival data ([Kvamme & Borgan 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06724)). Alongside it go the decayed history terms, his usual rate computed from earlier classes only, minute of class, lesson segment, current speaker, current silence length, time since the professor's last question, length of the current turn so far, speaker changes in the last 60 s, and his talk time so far. Some effects are non-linear, such as a probability that peaks a few seconds after a question and then decays, and a LightGBM fitted to the same person-period table captures these without any survival library. With 1–5% positives on 2-s bins, logit and complementary log-log links give nearly identical answers, so logit is the natural choice.

Classical and neural survival machinery fits poorly. Andersen–Gill and PWP recurrent-event Cox models describe the structure correctly but are built for inference, and lifelines' time-varying Cox documentation says plainly that "to predict, we would need to know the covariates values beyond the observed times" ([lifelines docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Time%20varying%20survival%20regression.html)). scikit-survival, DeepSurv and pycox assume one event per subject, and pycox has had no release since September 2024 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pycox/)). Neural temporal point processes condition only on past events, so the live transcript is invisible between raises. EasyTPP (0.3.0, July 2026) is maintained and PyTorch-only, but its data format has no slot for exogenous covariates ([EasyTPP](https://github.com/ant-research/EasyTemporalPointProcess)). The field's own review warns that negative log-likelihood "can be 'fooled'" when times are recorded in discrete units ([Shchur et al. 2021](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0623.pdf)), and history-independent baselines "solve" two popular benchmarks ([Enguehard et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13794)). tick 0.8.0.2, revived in 2026 with macOS arm64 wheels ([tick releases](https://github.com/X-DataInitiative/tick/releases)), is fine for a three-hour Hawkes baseline on raise history alone and nothing more.

## Text earns its place as cues and stacked scores, not end-to-end networks

### Lexical features and stacking set the recipe

The strongest evidence on mixing text with tabular features comes from an 18-dataset AWS benchmark ([Shi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.02705)).

| Text featurisation in Shi et al. | Average score |
|---|---|
| Frozen pretrained embeddings | 0.593 |
| Fine-tuned text networks | 0.638 |
| Stack ensemble of a multimodal model with trees (best) | 0.684 |

N-gram and word2vec features also beat the frozen embeddings, and late fusion beat early fusion. Those frozen embeddings predate modern contrastive embedders, so the gap has probably narrowed, but the recipe holds: keep TF-IDF logistic regression as a first-class text model, and feed its out-of-fold probabilities (grouped by class), plus those of an embedding model, into LightGBM next to the timing block.

### Floor-opening detectors are the highest-value text features

The ASR's own question mark is the cheapest cue. Deepgram says its punctuation draws on prosody such as rising intonation, which this pipeline otherwise lacks, but publishes no accuracy figure ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/speech-to-text-automatic-punctuation-guide)). Stanford SCALE's **EduBehaviors** release (September 2026) adds 49 small SetFit classifiers. On teacher utterances, `assertion_sentence_invites_participation` scores **F1 0.75, ROC-AUC 0.92 and AP 0.805 at a 24.3% base rate** ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/StanfordSCALE/assertion_sentence_invites_participation); [Bernado et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27043)), and sibling classifiers flag questions, rhetorical questions, whole-class address and calling on a student by name. A rhetorical question or a cold call to a named classmate should *suppress* raises, and a plain "?" cannot tell those apart. Stanford's Switchboard-trained question detector reached 0.967 validation accuracy with no history ([HF](https://huggingface.co/stanford-nlpxed/question-detection)). Classroom work shows question measures survive poor ASR: at **53.3% word accuracy, computer-coded question authenticity still correlated r = .687 with human coding** at the class level ([Kelly et al. 2018](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10175684)).

Generic dialogue-act taggers are a poor substitute. One trained on Switchboard labelled **77.2% of classroom utterances "statement-non-opinion" and only 0.457% yes-no questions** ([TalkMoves, LREC 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.497.pdf)). Names are better caught with a roster regex than a classifier. The conversational-uptake model matches human agreement (ρ = .54) ([Demszky et al. 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.130.pdf)), but it carries CC BY-NC-ND and no-share terms and lags a full turn, so it is optional.

### bge-small should replace MiniLM as the default embedder

MTEB computes its classification score by training logistic regression on frozen embeddings ([Muennighoff et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07316)), so it is the right proxy for "embedding plus linear head". **bge-small-en-v1.5** (33M parameters, MIT) scores 74.14 on MTEB v1 classification against 72.94 for e5-small-v2 ([BAAI card](https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5)), and 76.6 against 68.2 for IBM's granite-small on MTEB v2 ([Granite R2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21085)). all-MiniLM-L6-v2 remains the speed baseline, "5 times faster" than mpnet and still of "good quality" ([SBERT docs](https://www.sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html)).

The research team ran a local benchmark on this M4 on 12 Oct 2026 (16 GB, fp32, random weights, single run, roughly ±20%), which is the source of every measured M4 figure in this report. On MPS at 32 tokens it measured **2,921 sentences/s for MiniLM and 1,491/s for bge-small**, so embedding about 600k utterances once takes **about 3.5 and 7 minutes**. Re-embedding a million 60-s windows would take 45 minutes to 1.5 hours instead. The efficient pattern is to embed each utterance once, cache it, pool the cached vectors over the window with time decay, and reduce to 16–32 PCA dimensions fitted inside training folds before any tree sees them.

A person-specific feature asks "is this about something he wrote?": cosine similarity between the window and each of his submitted workbook answers. Quality matters more than speed here, because the answer set is tiny. google/embeddinggemma-2 (Apache 2.0, ungated, September 2026) or bge-base fit, but EmbeddingGemma-2 must not run in float16, which overflows to NaN ([model card](https://huggingface.co/google/embeddinggemma-2)). No study tests whether topic relevance predicts volunteering, so it is a hypothesis this project can test.

### End-to-end text models are expensive and thinly supported at this scale

The same M4 benchmark measured training throughput at batch 16.

| Model | Samples/s at 128 tokens | Samples/s at 256 tokens | Peak MPS memory at 256 tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| DistilBERT | 56 | 28 | about 4 GB |
| DeBERTa-v3-small | 28 | 11 | 8 GB |
| ModernBERT-base | 19 | 9 | about 8 GB |

ModernBERT loses its speed edge because MPS lacks the FlashAttention unpadding it relies on ([HF discussion](https://huggingface.co/answerdotai/ModernBERT-base/discussions/47)), and mixed precision gained under 5%. With very few samples, frozen sentence embeddings beat fine-tuning ([AACL 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.aacl-main.47.pdf)). What counts here is the few hundred to few thousand events, not the million rows. SetFit was built for 8–64 examples per class, and one user reported **F1 ≈ 0.18** with default settings at 0.8% positives ([setfit #161](https://github.com/huggingface/setfit/issues/161)). TextCNN "with little hyperparameter tuning and static vectors achieves excellent results" on sentence benchmarks ([Kim 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.5882)), but with limited labels it should land near TF-IDF logistic regression.

### Sequence and tabular foundation models get a tightly bounded try at most

Causal TCNs match or beat LSTMs on generic sequence tasks ([Bai et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01271)), and a GRU over utterance embeddings follows the hierarchical dialogue-encoder template ([Serban et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.04808)). But each class is one sequence, and 150–600 sequences support thousands to tens of thousands of parameters, not the million-plus of a transformer over utterances.

TabICLv2 (BSD-3, `tabicl` 2.2.0) is the open foundation-model comparator: it supports MPS, was tested to about 600k rows, and degrades beyond 100 features ([TabICL](https://github.com/soda-inria/tabicl)). TabPFN-3.5 accepts a million rows, but on CPU it caps use at 5,000 samples by default, limits MPS memory to 70%, and ships non-commercial weights behind a browser login ([TabPFN README](https://github.com/PriorLabs/TabPFN/blob/main/README.md)). Every "beats tuned gradient boosting" claim for either comes from the vendors themselves, run on H100s. Prior-fitted networks also degrade on rare classes, where thresholding works well and downsampling performs comparably ([McDowell et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21742)).

## The ranked menu: five musts, four shoulds, nine coulds

The ranking weighs fit to the evidence, cost on a 16 GB M4, and the risk of overfitting a few hundred events within four days. The musts form a complete, defensible pipeline on their own, the shoulds add the text-specific evidence, and the coulds are ablations to attempt only once the musts are evaluated. Intro baselines such as naive Bayes are judged in the verdict table that follows. "Measured" compute comes from the local M4 benchmark; everything else is an estimate. The project `.venv` (Python 3.12.4) currently has scikit-learn 1.9.0, pandas and NumPy but **not** lightgbm, statsmodels, torch, transformers, sentence-transformers, setfit or shap, so installing them is step zero.

## Leakage hides in timestamps, so audit them before comparing models

In a survey spanning 17 fields, Kapoor and Narayanan found that claims of complex models beating logistic regression in civil-war prediction all failed to reproduce once leakage was fixed ([Kapoor & Narayanan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048)). This project has more ways to leak than most, because every feature is a join on a timestamp. The table lists every place the research found future information slipping in.

| Where | What leaks | Guard |
|---|---|---|
| Offline ASR and diarization | Whole-recording ASR revises words, punctuation and speaker labels using later audio | Use only words with end time ≤ t minus a latency margin; Roddy et al. delayed word features by 100 ms to mimic real time ([Roddy et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.11461)); state that offline ASR is an optimistic stand-in for streaming |
| Utterance-level features ("?", classifier scores, embeddings) | Joined at utterance *start*, they expose words not yet spoken | Join on utterance *end* ≤ t; for an ongoing utterance use only the finalized prefix |
| Paired models (uptake, TalkMoves sentence pairs) | Need the reply to be complete | Lag until the pair completes |
| Talk after the raise | "Brady, go ahead", "I see a hand" and his own speech after being called are caused by the raise (leakage type L2, a proxy for the outcome) | Drop rows from onset until his hand goes down; inspect top n-grams and SHAP features for his name; add a guard band before onset if icon timing is uncertain |
| Converting hazard to p_H | The exact 1 − ∏(1 − h) uses future covariates | Freeze covariates at t, or fit a landmark model per H |
| Whole-class statistics | His total raises this class, class-mean z-scores, "fraction elapsed" computed from the actual end time | Use running statistics and the *scheduled* end time |
| Usual rate and normalisers | Computed over all classes, including test or future classes | Training classes only, and strictly earlier classes under forward chaining |
| Lesson segment | Inferred after the fact from later events | Use the segment as known at t, from the lesson plan or a segment-start event timestamped ≤ t |
| Fitting outside folds | TF-IDF vocabulary, IDF, PCA, scalers, resampling, calibrators, thresholds | Fit every one inside training-fold `Pipeline`s |
| Random row-level splits | Overlapping H windows put near-identical rows in train and test | GroupKFold by class plus forward chaining by date; report a random split only to measure the inflation |
| Stacking, kNN and in-context models | In-fold predictions; neighbours or context rows from the same session | Grouped out-of-fold predictions; training classes only |
| Sequence models | Bidirectional layers; normalisation over the whole session | Causal layers and running normalisation |
| Static content | Workbook answers submitted after class; pre-class material reused across sections | Filter by submission time; check near-duplicates or group splits by course unit |
| Label clock | An offset between hand-icon video time and ASR time can slide post-raise words before the label | Measure the offset and run the time-shift test |
| Forum `voice` events, if used for speaker timing | The event's `timestamp` marks the utterance *end* and carries its full duration (project `PLAN.md` §2) | Use only the portion of the utterance elapsed by t |

Every model should output the same quantity, p_H(t), at each decision point, so that hazard models, landmark classifiers and sequence models compete on equal terms. At the frame level, report average precision next to the prevalence and the lift (AP divided by prevalence), because a random ranker's AP equals the prevalence ([Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)). Report ROC-AUC alongside it, since AUPRC "is not generally superior" under imbalance ([McDermott et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06091)).

The headline should be event-level. The seizure-prediction framework defines a minimum lead time and an occurrence window, and tunes for maximum sensitivity under a cap on false alarms per hour ([Winterhalder et al. 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1525505003001057)). The Activity Monitoring Operating Characteristic, which plots false-alarm rate against time to detection, is the classic analogue ([Fawcett & Provost 1999](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/312129.312195)). Applied here, an alarm at time a hits onset t₀ when t₀ − H ≤ a ≤ t₀ minus the minimum useful lead; a small post-onset tolerance is defensible only to absorb icon-timing error and must be reported if used. A refractory period of about 10 s after each alarm stops one sustained high score from counting as many false alarms. False alarms are counted per *at-risk* minute, excluding time when his hand is up, he is speaking, or the class is in breakouts. For every model, and for a random alarmer at the same rate, report recall and median lead time at 0.25, 0.5 and 1 false alarm per minute. Never use point adjustment, which turns "even a random anomaly score" into an apparent state-of-the-art detector ([Kim et al. 2022](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/20680/20680-13-24693-1-2-20220628.pdf)).

Uncertainty must be estimated over sessions, never windows. Draw about 2,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples of whole sessions, and bootstrap *paired differences* when comparing models ([Field & Welsh 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00593.x)). For grouped cross-validation, the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test is the safer choice; in scikit-learn's own demonstration an uncorrected p of 0.005 becomes 0.227 after correction ([scikit-learn example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_grid_search_stats.html)). Naive cross-validation intervals undercover, and nested CV intervals run 20–80% wider ([Bates et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00673)). Pre-specify two primary comparisons, the best model against the hazard GLM and the full model against itself without transcript features, and apply Holm correction to those. Three sanity tests complete the audit: the gap between a random-row split and a grouped split; a label-permutation test, where shuffled onsets should drive AP to the base rate ([Ojala & Garriga 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/ojala10a.html)); and a deliberate time-shift that lets features peek 5–10 s ahead, where a large jump warns that the real model may be peeking too.

Train on the natural class distribution and move the threshold afterwards. In logistic-regression simulations, every imbalance correction left discrimination unchanged and pushed **median calibration intercepts to −4.5 or lower at a 1% event fraction**, while threshold moving matched their sensitivity and specificity ([van den Goorbergh et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09101)). For LightGBM, XGBoost and CatBoost, balancing did not help and threshold tuning was simpler ([Elor & Averbuch-Elor 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08528)). LightGBM's own documentation warns that its imbalance flags produce "poor estimates of the individual class probabilities" ([LightGBM docs](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Parameters.html)).

Calibrate with Platt scaling on held-out *sessions*: pass `CalibratedClassifierCV` an explicit list of grouped splits, or, under forward chaining, wrap the model in `FrozenEstimator` and calibrate on the previous date block. Avoid isotonic calibration, which scikit-learn advises against with "≪1000" calibration samples; here the effective count is events, not rows ([scikit-learn docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.calibration.CalibratedClassifierCV.html)). Temperature scaling suits the neural models ([Guo et al. 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)). Report the Brier *skill* score, because the constant forecaster's Brier score is only π(1 − π) ≈ 0.0196 at 2% prevalence and raw Brier scores flatter every model. Draw reliability diagrams with quantile bins, and treat fixed-bin ECE as an inefficient estimator ([Nixon et al. 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPRW_2019/papers/Uncertainty%20and%20Robustness%20in%20Deep%20Visual%20Learning/Nixon_Measuring_Calibration_in_Deep_Learning_CVPRW_2019_paper.pdf)). Calibration does not change AP or recall at a fixed false-alarm rate, and the write-up should say so.

For interpretability, the headline should be a table of feature-group ablations (timing-only, transcript-only, pre-class-only, and their combinations), each retrained on the same folds, because permute-and-predict importance "can be highly misleading" when features are correlated ([Hooker et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11222-021-10057-z)). TreeSHAP from LightGBM's `pred_contrib`, in log-odds ([LightGBM Booster API](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pythonapi/lightgbm.Booster.html)), top n-grams with their stability across folds, and kNN exemplars for individual hits and false alarms round out the story.

## Evidence is thinnest exactly where the big decisions sit

The central premise, that classroom discourse forecasts *this* student's raises, has no direct precedent, so every number in this report is borrowed from a neighbouring task. The turn-taking and next-speaker results come from dyadic or meeting speech, often with ground-truth transcripts and decisions made at utterance boundaries; on a fixed 2-s grid with partial utterances, performance should be lower than those papers report. No paper compares a stacked landmark classifier with a discrete-time hazard GLM on high-frequency recurrent behavioural events, so that choice must be settled empirically. No study compares modern frozen embedders such as bge or EmbeddingGemma against fine-tuned DistilBERT at about 1,000 positives with severe imbalance, and the main fusion benchmark predates both.

Every off-the-shelf discourse classifier was trained on K-12, mostly elementary-math, human-transcribed lessons, and none has been evaluated on university seminars or ASR output. The EduBehaviors cards and several talk-move cards state no licence. TabPFN and TabICL have only vendor benchmarks, all run on H100s, with no Apple Silicon timings. The M4 throughput figures are single-run fp32 measurements with random weights, not a published benchmark. No source measures how long after a question students raise hands in online seminars. No study covers calibration for autocorrelated streaming predictions, and the calibration evidence for trees under imbalance rests on one 2024 paper and one unreviewed 2026 preprint. No published application of conformal or risk-control methods to event-level false-alarm rates was found. Several participation findings, including the "half the variance is the student" result, rest on search snippets rather than full texts. None of this argues against the plan. It argues for treating every learned model as a hypothesis that the simple baselines can falsify.

## Conclusion

The sophistication in this project should come from the *formulation*, not from model depth. That means a hazard table that turns the H sweep into one coherent model, history terms that encode refractoriness, stacking that lets text in without swamping a few hundred events, and an alarm-style evaluation that measures what "seeing it coming" actually means. Each of those is beyond intro ML, defensible with citations, and cheap on an M4; transformers, at this sample size, are mostly none of those things.

The most informative result Brady can produce by Friday is the feature-group ablation. Next-speaker research shows that simple timing heuristics often match sophisticated text models, so if his transcript features add little over timing and history, that is the expected outcome and a credible finding. If the floor-opening cues or topic relevance add a measurable, leakage-audited lift, that is new evidence on a question no one has published.
